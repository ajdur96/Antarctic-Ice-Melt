How it works. The slider is shared with the Antarctica tab: the sea level rise from Antarctic melt is applied to the DC area. With the fingerprint on, DC gets 1.25× the global number, because losing Antarctica's gravitational pull pushes water toward the Northern Hemisphere (Mitrovica et al., 2009, Science). Water spreads only into ground connected to the tidal Potomac, the Anacostia or the map edge, so enclosed low spots stay dry until water tops their rim. A building gets water at its walls when the water reaches its lowest corner and is fully submerged when water passes its roof (highest ground + FEMA height; 6 m if unknown).

Simplifications. Antarctica only: no Greenland, glaciers or thermal expansion. No tides, storm surge, erosion or land subsidence. Elevations are NAVD88, roughly today's mean sea level.

Data. USGS 3DEP 1/3 arc-second DEM (10 m), NLCD 2021 land cover (30 m), FEMA/ORNL USA Structures (Region 3), Census TIGER 2010 state and county boundaries, all in UTM 18N.
How it works. Each area thins on its own schedule. Floating ice shelves go first, then low-lying, marine-based and more northerly ice (West Antarctica, the Peninsula, the coasts); the high East Antarctic plateau goes last, with some smooth random variation mixed in. The slider is the share of total ice volume melted. Sea level rise comes from the ice volume above flotation that is lost, spread over the world ocean (3.618 × 10⁸ km²). Ice floats wherever it is too thin to stay grounded below the new sea level. Elevations are shown relative to the new sea level.

Simplifications. The thaw order is an illustrative score, not a climate model. Ocean density, gravitational effects and the effect of rebound on sea level are ignored. Rebound uses full equilibrium (ρice/ρmantle ≈ 0.28), which takes thousands of years.

Data. Bedmap2 (Fretwell et al., 2013, The Cryosphere), resampled from 1 km to 5 km, Antarctic Polar Stereographic (EPSG:3031).
